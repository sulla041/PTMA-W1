\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

The advent of large language models (LLMs) is fundamentally transforming integrated circuit (IC) design, enabling automated and rapid generation of hardware description code from high-level functional specifications. However, realizing the promise of software-like agile hardware development—characterized by continuous, push-button design iterations—critically hinges on accelerating the physical evaluation loop, which currently represents the primary bottleneck. While generative models can produce register-transfer level (RTL) code almost instantaneously, evaluating circuit properties still requires prolonged turn-around times to run synthesis, placement, and timing analysis. To bridge this disparity, foundational circuit representation learning paradigms that can accurately and efficiently capture post-implementation characteristics are essential. Integrated circuits are naturally formulated as graphs, where logic cells and physical interconnections constitute nodes and edges endowed with rich and heterogeneous attributes, ranging from cell driving strengths to multi-dimensional routing parasitics. Moreover, predicting circuit characteristics inherently demands capturing long-range, multi-hop propagation dynamics across deep topological and causal logic chains. Consequently, pioneering dedicated graph learning algorithms specifically engineered to handle complex node-edge representations while scaling effectively across deep multi-hop circuit paths is of paramount theoretical and practical importance.

To model physical timing, signal dynamic switching, and routing propagation behaviors, circuit graph learning fundamentally requires capturing multi-hop topological dependencies across deep logic networks (as illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:intro_paradigm}, top). Under the conventional message-passing neural network (MPNN) paradigm~\cite{kipf2017gcn,velickovic2018gat}, gathering multi-hop structural context necessitates stacking a corresponding number of recursive message-passing layers. In realistic circuits, causal logic chains and signal paths routinely span dozens of topological levels, requiring dozens of sequential message-passing steps to trace these multi-hop dependencies. However, deep message-passing architectures inevitably suffer from the fundamental limitation of over-smoothing~\cite{rusch2023survey} (Figure~\ref{fig:intro_paradigm}, middle). Mathematically, recursive message passing acts as a continuous Laplacian smoothing operator: at each layer, node embeddings are updated through normalized weighted averages of their immediate neighbors. As the network depth grows to cover dozens of hops, this repeated spatial averaging contracts node features along the principal Dirichlet energy eigenspaces, driving distinct node embeddings toward an undifferentiated stationary mean. In circuit modeling, this mathematical collapse has catastrophic physical consequences: the distinct functional identities of logic gates and the sharp arrival-time differentials accumulated along downstream propagation paths are thoroughly washed out, rendering deep timing and causal reasoning ineffective. Furthermore, circuit interconnections encapsulate rich physical attributes that extend far beyond simple topological adjacency: interconnects carry driver impedances, fan-out load capacitances ($C_{\text{load}}$), and multi-dimensional routing parasitic RC networks. Most existing circuit graph algorithms either compress such multi-dimensional edge vectors into scalar attention coefficients or scalar transmission weights~\cite{velickovic2018gat,preroutgnn}, or construct cumbersome heterogeneous graphs that demand ad-hoc heuristic pre-processing and post-processing~\cite{timingpredict,deepgate2}. Such reductions discard vital physical interaction constraints, severely degrading modeling accuracy and restricting algorithmic generality across diverse EDA stages.

Our key insight posits that graph modeling is fundamentally a process of computing latent structural displacement over the embeddings. In this view, a node's native feature vector—obtained in isolation via its initial encoder—undergoes a structural offset when embedded within the complex topology of a circuit. To resolve the representational gap between disparate modalities, where nodes are individual vectors and edges are pair-wise vectors, we formulate a unified point-set representation. Rather than treating edges as external topological constraints or collapsed scalar weights, we map both nodes and relations into a unified latent space as a cohesive point set characterized by structural offsets (Figure~\ref{fig:intro_paradigm}, bottom). In this framework, every node expresses its embedding as a synthesis of its native properties and the accumulated structural displacement induced by graph topology. By reinterpreting the graph as a point set, we transform the circuit modeling problem from a heterogeneous message-passing challenge into a homogeneous set-modeling task, enabling seamless alignment of node and edge information through their collective contributions to latent space displacement.

We instantiate this conceptual framework through an end-to-end Trainable Interaction Dynamics Field. Departing from passive neighborhood averaging, our approach models point-set evolution by explicitly learning pairwise interaction patterns. We feed element-wise operations between intermediate node embeddings—coupled with complete multi-dimensional relation feature vectors—into a learnable dynamics function, which outputs all-directional displacement vectors in the latent space. This establishes an adaptive interaction field that dynamically modulates the state of each node based on the underlying graph topology. The reason this interaction field avoids over-smoothing across dozens of layers lies in its non-dissipative formulation: rather than performing convex combinations of neighbor states, our dynamics computes directional velocity offsets governed by learned pairwise operators and explicit sibling competition. As nodes evolve across dozens of interaction layers, the field performs continuous non-linear refinement, allowing for large structural displacements in initial layers to capture macro-connectivity and fine-grained adjustments in deeper layers to converge on high-fidelity features, all while preserving the high-frequency variance and discriminability of node embeddings. Furthermore, to capture whole-graph properties within this unified framework, we introduce a virtual-node graph information aggregation scheme. Specifically, measurement-specific virtual nodes are connected to the circuit graph as a specialized node type, with their incident edges marked as a specialized relation type. Both the virtual nodes and their associated edges are assigned corresponding feature vectors, naturally participating in the graph-to-point-set dynamic embedding process alongside regular nodes. Downstream tasks—spanning fine-grained node/pair characteristics to whole-graph metrics—are then accomplished directly by reading out the resulting embeddings.

The main contributions of this work are summarized as follows:
\begin{itemize}
    \item We propose a trainable interaction dynamics field model that maps the circuit graph into a point set and learns structural embeddings through pairwise interaction patterns, preventing over-smoothing across dozens of layers while enabling multi-dimensional relation features to participate directly in the dynamics.
    \item We propose a virtual-node graph information aggregation scheme, which unifies the extraction of whole-graph properties into the standard point-set embedding readout problem.
    \item We conduct extensive experiments across diverse downstream tasks on the open-source CircuitNet-3 and DeepGate benchmarks, validating the effectiveness and algorithmic generality of our proposed methodology.
\end{itemize}
